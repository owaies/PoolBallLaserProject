AI-Based Pool Ball Identification and Laser
Positioning System: A Systematic Survey
Aamir Awaiz Pasha, Ayan Khan, Ibad Sharief, and Mohammed Owaies
Department of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning
PES Institute of Technology and Management (PESITM)
Shivamogga, Karnataka, India
Abstract—Building an automated billiards training system
requires integrating computer vision, trajectory estimation, and
trajectory projection. Visual processing is difficult because of
specular reflections on balls and shadows near cushions. This
paper reviews 40 papers covering deep learning classifiers (U-
Net, YOLOv8, Xception), classical color segmentation, Circular
Hough Transforms, and physics-based models. We find that
while ball detection algorithms are highly accurate, on-table
coordinate calibration and steering hardware (like galvanometer-
mirror lasers) are rarely tested on real tables. Current systems
either use bulky gantry robots or show paths only on virtual
screens. Finally, we propose a development roadmap to link real-
time deep learning vision directly with active laser projection on
the felt.
Index Terms—Billiards, Computer vision, Object detection,
Deep learning, Trajectory tracking, Laser projection, Coordinate
calibration.
I. INTRODUCTION
Intelligent training assistants and pool-playing robots have
become practical due to advances in computer vision, state es-
timation, and visual feedback control. Creating an autonomous
billiards trainer requires combining three main components: a
vision system to detect the table layout, a physics engine to
calculate shot paths, and a mechatronic projection system to
display trajectories directly on the felt.
A. Domain Context and Engineering Motivation
Automating pool table analysis helps build interactive prac-
tice tools for players and competitive robots. However, this
involves several challenges. Visual sensors must detect the
table boundaries, pockets, and classify ball types (solids,
stripes, cue, and 8-ball) under ambient light shifts and surface
glares [1], [2], [5], [10]. Deep segmentors like U-Net and Mask
R-CNN are accurate but require high computation power [1],
while classical Circular Hough Transform (CHT) methods are
fast but fail near cushion shadows or clustered configurations
[2], [5], [8]. Additionally, the physics solver must predict
bounces and rebounds [20], [27], where aiming errors above
0.3 degrees result in missed pots [22]. Finally, coordinate
mapping requires camera calibration to compensate for lens
warp and camera tilt [13], [15], [19]. Hence, the vision and
projection modules must work in sync.
B. Need for a Systematic Literature Survey
Billiards automation research is currently fragmented. Most
computer vision studies focus only on static object detection or
segmentation, without testing physical actuators [1], [2], [3],
[5]. Meanwhile, precision laser steering papers test fast mirror
control on static patterns or medical setups rather than pool
tables [17], [18]. While pool-playing robots combine vision
and control, they use heavy gantry systems that are slow
and expensive [15], [16]. A review is needed to study how
deep learning vision, kinematic models, camera calibration,
and low-cost laser actuators can be integrated into a compact
embedded system.
C. Primary Contributions of this Survey
This paper reviews perception and hardware positioning
methods in cue sports. Our primary contributions are:
•Evaluation of AI Vision Pipelines: Comparing deep
learning models (U-Net, YOLOv8, Xception) and clas-
sical Hough/RGB approaches for ball detection.
•Synthesis of Trajectory Models: Comparing physics-
based equations, tracking filters, and reinforcement learn-
ing strategies based on aiming tolerance.
•Review of Calibration & Actuation: Analyzing ho-
mography mapping, camera calibration, and visual servo
loops for galvanometer-mirror steering.
•Identification of System Obstacles: Highlighting de-
ployment challenges like light variations, calibration drift,
and edge processing lag.

II. BACKGROUNDSTUDY
A. Computer Vision and Deep Learning for Ball Identification
Ball identification requires robust detection under varying
lighting. Deep learning models are now the standard approach.
Wang et al. [1] tested semantic segmentation on a pool
robot, finding that U-Net [30] outperforms Mask R-CNN [29].
Their U-Net achieved 0.998 mAP, 0.975 mAR, and 0.973
precision with 6.1 ms latency and a 121.3 MB memory size,
whereas Mask R-CNN needed 110 ms and 172.1 MB to reach
0.957 mAP. This U-Net pipeline helped their robot achieve
98.76% ball detection over 20 shots [1]. For ball and hand
tracking, Yang and Yan [11] and Yang [12] used YOLOv8
to localize cue tips, cue balls, and hands with 95% accuracy.
They noted that increasing resolution from 640 to 1280 pixels
improves mAP when using a Parallel Residual Bi-Fusion
Feature Pyramid Network (PRB-FPN) and self-distillation. For
depth data, Silva et al. [13] combined YOLOv8n with a Kinect
2 sensor, but accuracy decreased near cushions and under
low ceiling mounts. For classification, an Xception model
optimized with an Improved Chaos African Vulture Optimizer
(ICA VO) reached 98% shot accuracy without table rebounds
[3].
These systems draw from standard object detection back-
bones. Two-stage detectors like Faster R-CNN [33] are accu-
rate but limited to 5 FPS due to Region Proposal Network
(RPN) overhead. Single-stage detectors are faster: SSD [34]
reaches 59 FPS with 74.3% mAP on 300x300 inputs, while
YOLOv2 [32] runs at 67 FPS with 76.8 mAP. Anchor-free
models like CenterNet [35] treat objects as point centroids,
reaching 142 FPS on ResNet-18 backbones [38]. In cluttered
scenes, PoseCNN [36] estimates 6D poses by regressing
rotations and voting for 2D centers. In a sports ball tracking
review, Moreira et al. [40] highlighted that fast movement,
small size, and occlusions remain key challenges.
Classical vision is still used for calibration. Gao et al. [2]
combined RGB segmentation, Hough Transforms, and least-
squares circle fitting, achieving a 99.4% detection rate in
0.65 s, classifying balls with a CNN. Chua et al. [4] used
edge detection and CHT to map table corners and pockets.
The POOL-AID system [5] used color subtraction and affine
transforms to isolate the table, reaching 98.64% accuracy with
a maximum matching classifier, which dropped to 72.2% under
hand or cue occlusions. To handle specular glare, Park and
Park [10] averaged colors on an(N+ 1) 2 grid centered on
CHT centroids. In monocular setups, Uchiyama and Saito [7],
[8] isolated green felt to estimate positions, showing 18 mm
Y-axis errors due to felt boundary instability [7] and 10 mm
average error under handheld display tracking [8]. Zhang et al.
[9] used Canny filters and Hough transforms to calibrate table
lines on smartphones. However, classical methods are sensitive
to light changes [2], [5], [7], [13], specular highlights [1], [2],
[5], [10], and cushion shadows [8].
B. Ball Tracking and Trajectory Prediction Systems
Dynamic path prediction is required for shot planning.
Rodriguez-Lozano et al. [20] used a Multi-Object Local Track-
ing (MOLT) algorithm at 20 FPS, measuring accuracy with
MOTA and MOTP. In wearables, Stochasticks [25] estimates
paths using axial symmetry, Sobel edges, phase maps, and
wave propagation. Kinematics models are also common. Gab-
dulkhakova and Kropatsch [27] tracked snooker balls at 15–
29 FPS using 1D linear regression (y i =αx i +β+ϵ i). On
small single-board computers, OpenCV color trackers on a
Raspberry Pi [28] steered a pan-tilt camera to follow 17 cm
targets up to 200 cm away, though camera delays remain a
bottleneck.
For shot simulation, Park and Park [10] used PCA under
linear motion rules to estimate paths under noise. Analytical
equations, like those by Kim [21], model sliding, rolling, spin,
and cushion bounces. Kiefl [24] developed Pooltool, a Python
simulation package. These models are crucial for planning;
Chen and Li [22] built a reinforcement learning agent, showing
that aiming errors above 0.3 degrees lead to missed pots.
Zheng and Yan [23] introduced Attention-Pool, using Object
Attention and Temporal Context Gated Attention (TCGA) over
58,078 frames, improving potted ball classification by 6.2%.
In robotics, Nierhoff et al. [14], [16] suggested shots using
discounted return optimization [14] and simulated player skill
using Markov Decision Processes (MDP) to adapt robotic play
[16]. In practice, trajectory accuracy is limited by motion blur
[20], occlusions [20], [23], felt friction drift [13], [16], and
small aiming offsets [22].
C. Hardware Integration and Laser/Projection Guidance Sys-
tems
Linking vision to hardware projection requires accurate
spatial mapping. Zhang [19] showed that under noiseσ= 0.5,
relative focal length errors are below 0.3%, with center point
errors within 1 pixel. Long et al. [15] built a pool robot
using top-down and end-effector cameras, mapping 2D pixels
to a 3D robot workspace to reach 0.6 mm accuracy and
a 67% potting rate. Schiøtt et al. [6] mapped ball centers
using homography with a mean error of 0.4 cm. Monocular
camera calibration by Uchiyama and Saito [7], [8] achieved
homography mapping with 18 mm maximum error [7] and 10
mm average error [8]. Park and Park [10] minimized back-
projection error to align DLP projectors.
Visual servoing converts pixel coordinates to motor in-
puts. Chaumette and Hutchinson [39] compared Image-Based
(IBVS) and Position-Based Visual Servoing (PBVS), noting
that IBVS runs on the 2D image plane while PBVS computes
errors in 3D camera space. For laser targeting, Odira et al. [17]
used visual servoing to steer a beam via galvanometer mirrors.
By minimizing geodesic cross-product errors, they achieved
exponential convergence without camera-mirror calibration.
Liu et al. [18] used a visual/PSD controller to reach 30
=== PAGE 3 ===
µm positioning accuracy. For training, Kang and Choi [26]
developed an AR trainer using stereo cameras to link table
states to a Unity engine simulation. Real-world setups are
limited by IBVS local minima [39], PBVS calibration noise
[39], motor speed limits [14], [16], and optical sensor costs
[18].
III. RESEARCHOBJECTIVES
To address the limits of automated billiards assistants, we
evaluate research across five technical goals.
A. Objective 1: Optimized Image Acquisition and Optical
Setup
The first goal is capturing high-contrast, synchronized video
of the table. Top-down overhead cameras provide global,
unoccluded views [1], [2], [15]. Handheld monocular cameras
[7], [8] and smartphone sensors [9] are highly flexible but
introduce perspective tilt and lens distortion. Dynamic trackers
need high frame rates to capture fast rebounds [20], [27], while
wearables use head-mounted HUDs to align trajectories with
the user’s line of sight [25].
B. Objective 2: Automated Table Detection and Spatial Cali-
bration
The second goal is detecting table boundaries and au-
tomating spatial calibration. Vision systems locate the felt
and cushion edges using line transforms [4], edge filters
[9], or color extraction [7]. To transform pixel coordinates
(u, v)to physical table coordinates(X, Y), systems calculate
homography matricesHusing checkerboards [19] or pocket
reference points [6], [10].
C. Objective 3: Robust AI Ball Detection, Classification, and
Identification
The third goal is identifying all ball classes despite glare
and lighting variations. We evaluate neural segmentors (U-Net,
Mask R-CNN [1]), YOLO models [11]– [13], and Xception
classifiers [3] against classical Hough and color subtraction
methods [2], [5], [10], comparing detection rates and latency
under occlusions.
D. Objective 4: Low-Latency Real-Time Ball Tracking and
Trajectory Estimation
The fourth goal is tracking moving balls and simulating
paths in real time. Planners use multi-object trackers at 20 FPS
[20], 1D regression [27], or Kalman filters on single-board
computers [28]. The tracking data feeds into sliding/rolling
kinematic models [21], event-based simulators [24], or rein-
forcement learning planners [22] to calculate paths.
E. Objective 5: High-Precision Physical Laser Positioning
and Hardware Projection
The fifth goal is projecting shot paths directly onto the
table felt instead of screens or VR headsets [8], [25], [26].
Robotic cue systems use gantry frames for positioning [15].
Alternatively, active laser systems drive galvanometer mirrors
via visual servo loops to steer beams with exponential error
convergence [17], or combine cameras with PSD sensors for
micrometer-level precision [18].
IV. PROBLEMSTATEMENT
Designing a real-time billiards assistant requires correcting
spatial calibration errors, reducing visual processing lag, and
connecting perception algorithms to physical projection actu-
ators.
A. Context and the Core Engineering Conflict
A major issue is the trade-off between inference time and
classification accuracy. Deep segmentors like Mask R-CNN
[29] are highly accurate but have a 110 ms latency and a 172.1
MB memory size [1], making them too slow for real-time edge
processing. Meanwhile, single-stage networks (YOLO [31]–
[33], CenterNet [35]) and classical Hough setups [2], [5] run
faster but struggle under reflections on polished ball surfaces
[2], [10], and player hand or cue stick occlusions, which drop
detection rates from 98.64% to 72.2% [5].
B. The Hardware-Software Integration Deficit
Most studies develop perception algorithms in isolation
from physical hardware control. Shot recommendations are
usually displayed on screens or VR systems [3], [5], [6],
[8], [26] rather than on the physical felt. Conversely, high-
precision mechatronics like mirror visual servoing [17] or
hybrid visual/PSD controllers [18] (reaching 30µm accuracy)
are tested only in industrial or medical workspaces. Robots
that integrate vision and mechatronics rely on slow, expensive
gantry frames that suffer from mechanical backlash, capping
potting success at 67% [15].
C. Formal Synthesis of the Problem Statement
We define the core bottleneck as the lack of a low-latency,
low-cost system linking real-time vision to physical laser
steering. Standard overhead setups suffer from camera tilt and
mounting vibrations, causing up to 18 mm tracking offsets on
the felt [7]. This offset is critical because aiming errors above
0.3 degrees cause missed pots on cushion rebounds [22].
This error is worsened by synchronization delays between
camera capture and servo actuators, leading to frame drops
on single-board hardware like the Raspberry Pi [28]. To
solve this, we need an integrated architecture combining a
fast neural network (e.g., U-Net running in 6.1 ms [1]) with
a closed-loop dynamic homography module [19] to drive a
mirror-galvanometer directly, removing mechanical play and
calibration drift.

Papers were selected if they evaluated: (i) machine learning

without experimental results, hardware descriptions, or visual
table mapping were excluded.
B. Categorization and Taxonomy Framework
The selected papers are organized across four technical axes:
1)Ball Identification Paradigm: Contrasts deep semantic
segmentors (U-Net [1], Mask R-CNN [29]), single-stage
detectors (YOLO [6], [11]– [13], [31], [32], CenterNet
[35]), and classical vision (CHT, edge filters, color
thresholds [2], [4], [5], [8], [10]).
2)Tracking and Trajectory Estimation: Evaluates path
solvers, including linear estimators (Kalman filters [28],
1D regression [27], PCA [10]), reinforcement learning
agents (DQN/PPO [6], [22]), and physics simulators
(Pooltool [24], analytical friction equations [21]).
3)Hardware and Sensor Integration: Groups configura-
tions by camera and display setups, including top-down
cameras [1], [2], [4], [5], [10]– [13], mobile monocular
views [6]– [9], robotic arms [15], [18], galvanometer
mirrors [17], and head-mounted displays [25], [26].
4)Actuator Guidance and Control Mode: Compares trajec-
tory projection methods, including virtual AR overlays
[7], [8], [25], [26], closed-loop servoing (IBVS/PBVS)
[39], mirror geodesic alignment [17], and dual vi-
sual/PSD controllers [18].
This categorization allows us to compare systems based on
latency, coordinate precision, and hardware limits.
C. Comparative Evaluation Methodology
Comparing performance across studies is difficult due to
varying evaluation metrics. We compare systems using: (i)
classification accuracy/mAP; (ii) spatial positioning error (in
mm or cm); (iii) latency/frame rates (in ms or FPS); and (iv)

pointing or motor positioning error. Missing parameters (e.g.,
resolution, GPU hardware, calibration details) are marked as
“Not Reported”. For example, while Wang et al. [1] report U-
Net vs. Mask R-CNN latency (6.1 ms vs. 110 ms), other papers
[4], [5], [8] use visual validation without reporting positioning
errors. Calibration precision is compared using focal length
error (under 0.3% in [19]), average robotic positioning error
(0.6 mm in [15]), and visual servoing accuracy (30µm in
[18]).

A. Literature Synthesis Summary
Analysis of the 40 surveyed papers reveals a clear focus
on software over physical hardware testing. Specifically, 85%
of the reviewed studies (34 papers) concentrate strictly on
computer vision algorithms, ball classification, and strategy
simulations. Only 15% (6 papers: [8], [10], [15], [17], [18],
[26]) build or evaluate physical projection setups, mirror steer-
ing assemblies, or robotic actuators. Among vision strategies,
single-stage deep networks (such as YOLOv8 and CenterNet)
and traditional Circular Hough Transforms (CHT) remain the
primary methods used.
Reporting practices across these studies also show gaps in
physical testing. Out of 40 papers, only 5 (12.5%) record
physical positioning errors in metric units on the table surface
(e.g., 0.6 mm for gantry robots [15], 0.4 cm for homography
mapping [6], 10–18 mm for handheld monocular cameras [7],
[8], and 30µm for high-precision PSD servoing [18]). The
remaining 35 papers evaluate their outputs visually or skip
spatial error measurements altogether. While model inference
times are frequently reported (ranging from 6.1 ms for U-Net
to 110 ms for Mask R-CNN [1]), hardware delays between
camera capture and physical projection steering are rarely
measured.
These gaps point to the need for unified, low-latency ar-
chitectures that remain reliable under varying light conditions.
Meeting this goal requires pairing real-time neural segmen-
tors with closed-loop dynamic calibration to drive high-speed
galvanometer laser modules directly on the pool table felt.

C. Deep-Dive Sub-Section Analysis
1) Sensing and Optical Acquisition Modality:Visual sen-
sor choice dictates system resilience to light variations and
spatial distortion. Central top-down RGB cameras are the
most common layout [1]–[5], [10], [14], providing a stable
view of the table. Portable monocular cameras are flexible
but introduce perspective tilt and lens warping [7]–[9]. To
manage occlusions and dynamic alignments, stereo or multi-
camera rigs are used in visual servo loops [15], [17], [18],
[26]. Dedicated depth mapping is rare, with Kinect sensors
being the only example [13].
2) Computer Vision and Ball Identification Architecture:
Perception models are split between traditional geometric
algorithms and deep neural networks. Classical methods com-
bine CHT edge detection and HSV color thresholding [2], [4],
[5], [10], but specular highlights on glossy ball surfaces cause
centroid errors [2], [10]. Deep learning networks, especially
YOLO variants [11]–[13] and semantic models like U-Net and
Mask R-CNN [1], handle occlusions well, but their execution
delay must be budgeted on edge hardware [1].

3) Calibration and Coordinate Transformation Strategy:
Steering a laser requires mapping image pixels(u, v)to
physical felt coordinates(X, Y). Manual calibration uses static
templates [9], which are vulnerable to camera displacement
or table vibrations. Automated methods compute homography
matrices directly from table pockets and cushion lines [5]–
[8], [10]. Precise positioning requires compensating for camera
lens distortion and 3D out-of-plane parallax [15], [18], [19].
4) Ball Tracking and Dynamic Trajectory Modeling:Plan-
ning valid shots requires tracking balls across video frames.
Kinematic filters (such as Kalman filters, mean-shift trackers,
and multi-object MOLT pipelines) calculate velocity vectors
and spin rates [7], [8], [20], [28]. Advanced systems feed coor-

kinematics models to simulate slide-to-roll transitions, cushion
rebounds, and collision energy loss [6], [21], [22], [24].

Trajectory display systems are split between virtual and phys-
ical setups. Most existing papers display paths on virtual
monitors or screens [1], [20], [23], leaving the physical felt
unmarked. Projector-camera platforms project static lines onto
the table felt using DLP projectors [10], [26]. Physical actua-
tors use 3-axis gantry frames to position mechanical cues [15],
or drive mirror galvanometers in closed visual loops using PSD
feedback for active coordinate alignment [17], [18].

A. Image Acquisition and Lighting Sensitivity Gaps
Visual systems are highly sensitive to ambient light shifts.
Most setups use global overhead cameras [1]–[5], [10]–[12]
assuming uniform, static lighting. Three visual issues remain
unresolved:
•Specular Reflection Glare:High-gloss ball surfaces
reflect overhead lamps, causing glare that breaks CHT
edge continuity [2], [10]. Gao et al. [2] noted that glare
distorts color-space classifiers, yet polarized filters or
glare-invariant networks are not tested.
•Ambient Illumination Shifts:Color segmentors and
HSV classifiers fail under changing venue lighting [3],
[5]. Pan et al. [3] used chaos optimization, but ambient
shifts still trigger misclassifications.
•Mounting Vibration and Jitter:Mechanical impacts
transmit vibration to ceiling camera mounts. Park and
Park [10] observed homography drift under table vibra-
tion, but active vibration compensation is missing.
B. Table Calibration and Coordinate Transformation Short-
comings
Mapping camera pixels(u, v)to physical coordinates
(X, Y)on the felt presents three main limitations:
•Static Calibration Assumptions:Most models [1]–[3],
[11]–[13], [28] assume table boundaries stay fixed, re-
quiring manual recalibration if the camera shifts [9].
•Lens Distortion and Parallax:Standard 2D homography
[6]–[8], [10] neglects lens radial distortion. For monoc-
ular handy cameras, Uchiyama and Saito [7], [8] report
up to 18 mm Y-axis errors due to edge tilt. Correcting
this requires full 3D distortion models [15], [18], [19].
•Calibration Ambiguity:Planar grids suffer from projec-
tion ambiguities and degenerate states when the camera
tilt approaches zero [19].
C. AI Ball Identification and Occlusion Classification Bottle-
necks
Vision models face three structural bottlenecks in dense,
active gameplay:
•Clustered Occlusions:When balls touch, single-stage
and two-stage detectors [11]–[13], [31]–[35] suffer
bounding box overlaps and localization errors. Bauza et
al. [5] report cue/hand occlusions drop ball identification
accuracy from 98.64% to 72.2%.
•Missing 3D Rotation State:No surveyed model tracks
the 3D rotational orientation of striped balls. They assume
balls are static spheres and misclassify striped balls when
the pattern rotates away from the camera.
•Compute Latency on Edge Hardware:Wang et al. [1]
noted U-Net achieves 6.1 ms latency but lacks detail,
while Mask R-CNN [29] achieves strong accuracy but
takes 110 ms per frame, which is too slow for real-time
laser steering.
D. Real-Time Trajectory Prediction and Physics Engine Inte-
gration Deficits
The gap between static image detection and dynamic phys-
ical shot execution is limited by:
•Static Analysis Limits:Most deep learning systems [1]–
[5], [9], [11]–[13] evaluate only single static frames,
omitting velocity vectors, spin rates, and collision times-
tamps.

loss. Aiming offsets above 0.3 degrees cause shot failure
in physical systems [22], yet remain uncalibrated.
•Visual Interference:Cue sticks and hands introduce
moving distractors that confuse trackers [20], [27].

X. CHALLENGES ANDLIMITATIONS
Key systemic challenges restricting real-world deployment
include:
A. High Computational and Power Constraints
Running deep semantic segmentors (like U-Net or Mask R-
CNN) on compact single-board devices (such as a Raspberry
Pi) requires significant network quantization and pruning to
achieve acceptable frame rates.
B. Adversarial and Visual Susceptibility
Vision pipelines remain sensitive to practical disturbances.
Moving hands, cue sticks, or changing shadows on the felt
disrupt edge detection and cause false targets in neural classi-
fiers.
C. Data Long-Tail Distribution
Building comprehensive training datasets for edge cases—
such as tightly clustered balls, steep reflection angles, or worn
felt textures—remains labor-intensive and costly.
XI. FUTURERESEARCHDIRECTIONS
To address these challenges, future efforts should focus on:
A. Neuromorphic Edge Computing
Exploring event-driven, spike-based sensors to achieve
ultra-low processing delays (<1ms) and reduced power
consumption, enabling high-speed collision tracking without
motion blur.
B. Foundation Models for Cue Sports
Developing self-supervised vision models capable of trans-
ferring across different pool tables, felt colors, and lighting
setups without manual recalibration.
C. Formal Mechatronic Verification
Combining control barrier functions (CBFs) with neural
trajectory planners to ensure safe, stable operation during high-
speed galvanometer laser steering.
XII. CONCLUSION
This survey has reviewed computer vision, trajectory predic-
tion, and physical guidance methodologies in autonomous cue-
sport systems. By establishing a 5-axis taxonomy, compiling
a 40-paper comparison matrix, and evaluating hardware trade-
offs, this work outlines key progress and persistent limitations
in the field. Resolving existing gaps in light sensitivity, edge
processing delays, and physical projection calibration will be
essential to building practical, low-cost training assistants.
